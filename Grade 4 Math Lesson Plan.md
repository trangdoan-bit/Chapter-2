# Grade 4 Math Lesson Plan
## Chapter 2 – Lessons 2.1–2.2: Multiplication Comparisons & Comparison Problems

**Duration:** 45 minutes  
**Grade:** 4  
**Subject:** Mathematics  
**Topic:** Multiplicative Comparison  
**Standards:** 4.OA.A.1, 4.OA.A.2

---

## 1. Learning Objectives

By the end of the lesson, students will be able to:

- interpret expressions such as **“3 times as many as”** as multiplicative comparisons;
- represent multiplicative comparisons using a **bar model, comparison sentence, and equation**;
- identify the unknown quantity in a comparison problem;
- solve simple word problems involving multiplicative comparisons;
- distinguish between **“times as many”** and **“more than.”**

### Success Criteria

Students can:

- [ ] explain what “times as many” means;
- [ ] draw a comparison model correctly;
- [ ] write a matching multiplication equation;
- [ ] solve a comparison word problem and explain their reasoning.

## 4. Lesson Procedure

### A. Warm-up – Activate Prior Knowledge
**Time: 5 minutes**

Display:

**3 × 5 = 15**

Ask students:

1. What are the factors?
2. What is the product?
3. How could we describe this equation using words?

Introduce:

> **15 is 3 times as many as 5.**

Then ask:

> Is “3 times as many” the same as “3 more than”?

Use a quick example:

- 3 more than 5 = 8
- 3 times as many as 5 = 15

Emphasize that **multiplicative comparison is different from additive comparison**.

---

### B. Mini-Lesson – Lesson 2.1: Multiplication Comparisons
**Time: 10 minutes**

Present the textbook-style problem:

> Carly has 9 pennies. Jack has 4 times as many pennies as Carly. How many pennies does Jack have?

### Step 1 – Identify the quantities

Carly = 9 pennies

Jack = 4 times Carly’s amount

### Step 2 – Draw a bar model

Carly:

`[ 9 ]`

Jack:

`[ 9 ][ 9 ][ 9 ][ 9 ]`

### Step 3 – Write an equation

**n = 4 × 9**

**n = 36**

Therefore:

> Jack has **36 pennies**.

Ask:

- What does the number **4** represent?
- What does the number **9** represent?
- What does **36** represent?

### Key Concept

A multiplicative comparison tells us how many times greater one quantity is than another.

---

### C. Guided Practice – From Model to Equation
**Time: 8 minutes**

Solve together:

> There are 8 students in the art club. There are 3 times as many students in chorus. How many students are in chorus?

Students complete:

**Model**

Art club:

`[ 8 ]`

Chorus:

`[ 8 ][ 8 ][ 8 ]`

**Equation**

n = 3 × 8

n = 24

**Comparison sentence**

> There are 3 times as many students in chorus as in the art club.

Then reverse the representation.

Give students:

**7 × 5 = 35**

Ask them to write a comparison sentence.

Expected:

> 35 is 7 times as many as 5.

or

> 7 times as many as 5 is 35.

---

### D. Lesson 2.2 – Solving Comparison Problems
**Time: 10 minutes**

Move from direct comparisons to problems involving a **total**.

Present:

> Maria’s dog weighs 6 times as much as her rabbit. Together, the pets weigh 56 pounds. How much does Maria’s dog weigh?

Explain that this problem is more challenging because the smaller amount is unknown.

### Step 1 – Draw the model

Rabbit:

`[ n ]`

Dog:

`[ n ][ n ][ n ][ n ][ n ][ n ]`

Total = 56

There are **7 equal parts** altogether.

### Step 2 – Find one part

7 × n = 56

n = 8

Rabbit = 8 pounds

### Step 3 – Find the dog's weight

6 × 8 = 48

Therefore:

> Maria’s dog weighs **48 pounds**.

### Teacher Check

Ask:

> Why did we divide the total into 7 equal parts instead of 6?

Expected understanding:

> There are 6 parts for the dog plus 1 part for the rabbit.

---

### E. Pair Challenge – Model, Solve, Explain
**Time: 7 minutes**

Students work in pairs.

### Problem

> At the zoo, there are 3 times as many monkeys as lions. There are 24 monkeys and lions altogether. How many monkeys are there?

Students must:

1. identify the smaller quantity;
2. draw a bar model;
3. write an equation;
4. solve;
5. explain their answer to their partner.

### Expected solution

Lion:

`[ n ]`

Monkeys:

`[ n ][ n ][ n ]`

4 × n = 24

n = 6

Monkeys:

3 × 6 = 18

**Answer: 18 monkeys**

### Extension

Ask early finishers:

> How many more monkeys are there than lions?

18 − 6 = 12

---

### F. Exit Ticket
**Time: 5 minutes**

Students complete independently.

### Question 1

Write a comparison sentence for:

**6 × 4 = 24**

### Question 2

> Ben has 4 times as many stickers as Anna. Anna has 5 stickers. How many stickers does Ben have?

### Question 3

> Tom has 3 times as many books as Mia. Together they have 20 books. How many books does Tom have?

### Answers

1. 24 is 6 times as many as 4.
2. 4 × 5 = **20 stickers**
3. 4 equal parts = 20 → one part = 5 → Tom = 3 × 5 = **15 books**

## 5. Assessment

### Formative Assessment

During the lesson, observe whether students can:

- interpret “times as many” correctly;
- distinguish multiplicative from additive comparison;
- draw bar models;
- identify the unknown;
- translate models into equations.

### Exit Ticket

Use the three exit-ticket questions to classify students:

**Secure:** 3/3 correct  
**Developing:** 2/3 correct  
**Needs Support:** 0–1/3 correct

---

## 6. Differentiation

### Support

For students who need additional support:

- use concrete counters or cubes;
- provide partially completed bar models;
- highlight key phrases such as **“times as many”** and **“together”**;
- use smaller multiplication facts;
- provide sentence frames.

Example:

> ___ is ___ times as many as ___.

### Challenge

For advanced students:

- provide problems where the smaller quantity is unknown;
- include total-and-comparison problems;
- ask students to calculate the difference between the quantities;
- ask students to create their own comparison problem for a partner.

---

## 7. Common Misconceptions

### Misconception 1

Students interpret:

> “4 times as many as 3”

as:

> 4 + 3.

**Teacher response:** Compare:

- 4 more than 3 = 7
- 4 times as many as 3 = 12

---

### Misconception 2

Students multiply the total by the comparison factor in a Lesson 2.2 problem.

**Teacher response:** Return to the bar model and count the total number of equal parts first.

---

### Misconception 3

Students confuse the larger and smaller quantities.

**Teacher response:** Ask:

> “Which quantity has more equal parts?”
